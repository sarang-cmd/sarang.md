# Circles, radians and sectors

> **SL 3.4 · AA SL foundation (also studied at HL)** · Original Sarang.md course lesson, not an IB publication.

## Understand the idea

A radian measures angle through arc length relative to radius. This makes sector formulas simple, because angle becomes a dimensionless ratio. A full revolution is $2\pi$ radians, so check that any sector angle is in the correct units before substitution.

## Formula, meaning and conditions

For radius $r$ and central angle $\theta$ **in radians**, arc length $s=r\theta$ and sector area $A=\tfrac12r^2\theta$. In degrees, first convert by $\theta_{\rm rad}=\theta_{\rm deg}\pi/180$.

**Booklet cross-check:** The supplied IBO 2023, Mathematics: analysis and approaches HL formula booklet, Version 1.0, printed p. 5 (PDF p. 7), lists arc length and sector area for radians under SL 3.4. Derive and justify all other steps and conditions.

## Worked example

A sector has radius $6$ cm and angle $\pi/3$. Its arc length is $6(\pi/3)=2\pi$ cm. Its area is $\tfrac12(6)^2(\pi/3)=6\pi$ cm$^2$. As a check, $\pi/3$ is one sixth of a full circle, and one sixth of $36\pi$ is $6\pi$.

## Smaller skills within this topic

Practise each skill separately, then combine them in problems:

1. Convert degrees to radians and back.
2. Find arc length, sector area and perimeter using radians.
3. Determine an angle from a measured arc or area without mixing units.

## Why the method works

A radian is defined by an arc length equal to the radius. Consequently, an angle $\theta$ in radians measures arc-to-radius ratio and gives $s=r\theta$. Sector area follows by taking the same fraction of a full circle. Both formulas require radians as input; a bare degree measure must be converted before substitution.

## A contrasting worked route

For radius $6$ and angle $\pi/3$, arc length is $2\pi$ and sector area is $\tfrac12(36)(\pi/3)=6\pi$. Sector perimeter is $12+2\pi$, because two radii must be included. Using $60$ directly in the radian formula would overstate the answer dramatically.

## Transfer and validation

The central angle can exceed $2\pi$ when modeling multiple revolutions, but a conventional single sector usually uses one full turn or less. Draw and label the radii to distinguish the curved boundary from the whole perimeter. Attach linear or squared units appropriately.

## Exam lens

Paper 1 style: include units and label radians; a diagram can justify which arc belongs to a perimeter. This is a practice lens, not a prediction of an examination question.

## A mistake worth catching

Putting $60$ into $s=r\theta$ without converting degrees produces an answer about ten times too large. Use $\pi/3$ instead.

## Try it yourself

Find the arc length of a $4$ cm radius sector with angle $\pi/2$.

## Checked answer

$s=4(\pi/2)=2\pi$ cm.
