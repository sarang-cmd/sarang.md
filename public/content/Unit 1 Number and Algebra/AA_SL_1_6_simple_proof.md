# Simple proof

> **SL 1.6 · AA SL foundation (also studied at HL)** · Original Sarang.md course lesson, not an IB publication.

## Understand the idea

A proof establishes a statement throughout its stated domain. It starts from definitions or accepted results and uses valid implications. Testing several integers may suggest a pattern but cannot prove a universal claim.

## Formula, meaning and conditions

For divisibility, exhibit an integer factor: $m\mid N$ means $N=mk$ for some integer $k$. For an identity, simplify one side under the stated domain; do not assume the identity you are trying to prove.

## Worked example

Prove that the sum of three consecutive integers is divisible by $3$. Let the integers be $n,n+1,n+2$, where $n\in\mathbb Z$. Their sum is $3n+3=3(n+1)$. Because $n+1$ is an integer, the sum is a multiple of $3$ for every allowed $n$. The quantifier and the integer condition complete the argument.

## Why the method works

The phrases “for all” and “there exists” change what counts as evidence. To prove every integer with a property satisfies a claim, represent an arbitrary member of that class and derive the conclusion. One counterexample suffices to refute a universal assertion, but a list of successful examples proves only those instances. State the allowable integers at the outset.

## A contrasting worked route

For any integer $n$, $n^2+n=n(n+1)$ is even because one of two consecutive integers is even. This argument covers negative integers too; checking $n=1,2,3$ would not. If a claim says every prime is odd, $2$ is a counterexample. Notice that disproof requires a valid member of the domain, not merely a convenient number.

## Transfer and validation

When writing a proof, audit each equality from left to right and identify which property licenses it. An implication cannot automatically be reversed. After establishing a claim, explain why the final expression meets the definition you started from. This habit prepares for induction and contradiction without confusing their roles.

## Exam lens

Paper 1 style: a clear general representation and a final sentence often matter more than long calculations. This is a practice lens, not a prediction of an examination question.

## A mistake worth catching

Checking $1+2+3$ verifies one instance, not all instances. Replace specific values with an arbitrary integer $n$.

## Try it yourself

Show that the difference of the squares of consecutive integers is odd.

## Checked answer

$(n+1)^2-n^2=2n+1$, which is odd for every integer $n$.
