# Graph features and intersections

> **SL 2.4 · AA SL foundation (also studied at HL)** · Original Sarang.md course lesson, not an IB publication.

## Understand the idea

To find where two functions meet, equate their outputs at the same input. Technology can estimate crossing coordinates, but the algebra and the viewing window determine whether the reported solutions are complete. Distinguish a local feature from a global one.

## Formula, meaning and conditions

Intersections satisfy $f(x)=g(x)$. For a polynomial, turning points have horizontal tangents, but a stationary point may be a maximum, a minimum or neither. For a rational function, discontinuities can interrupt a graphical trace.

## Worked example

Find the intersections of $y=x^2$ and $y=2x+3$. Equate: $x^2=2x+3$, so $x^2-2x-3=(x-3)(x+1)=0$. With $x=3$, $y=9$; with $x=-1$, $y=1$. The crossing points are $(3,9)$ and $(-1,1)$. A calculator plot should show both if its window includes them.

## Smaller skills within this topic

Practise each skill separately, then combine them in problems:

1. Read intercepts, turning points, monotonic intervals and asymptotes.
2. Identify intersections of two functions rather than of one function with an axis.
3. Use technology to estimate features, then verify with algebra where possible.

## Why the method works

An intersection solves $f(x)=g(x)$, whereas an $x$-intercept solves $f(x)=0$. A turning point is a local behavior statement and may not be the global extreme on a restricted domain. The number and location of solutions can depend on the viewing window; tabulate sign changes or calculate exact roots when the expressions allow it.

## A contrasting worked route

The line $y=x+1$ meets the parabola $y=x^2-1$ where $x^2-x-2=0$, giving $x=-1$ and $x=2$. Their coordinates are $(-1,0)$ and $(2,3)$. Merely reporting $x=-1,2$ omits the required points, and replacing the line by the $x$-axis would answer a different question.

## Transfer and validation

Use a graph to frame a conjecture about roots or asymptotes, not as the sole proof of an exact identity. Mark open endpoints on a restricted domain and distinguish an included boundary maximum from an interior turning point. Report approximate coordinates with justified precision when a numerical solver is needed.

## Exam lens

Paper 2 style: record coordinates and a calculation or graph method, then check each point in both functions. This is a practice lens, not a prediction of an examination question.

## A mistake worth catching

Reporting only the $x$-coordinates does not give intersection points. Substitute back to find the corresponding $y$-values.

## Try it yourself

Where do $y=x+2$ and $y=2x-1$ intersect?

## Checked answer

$x+2=2x-1$ gives $x=3$, then $y=5$: $(3,5)$.
